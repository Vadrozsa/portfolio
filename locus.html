Case study

Locus

A community-driven platform designed to ease menstrual poverty in Budapest while empowering women to become more conscious, informed, and connected.

Context
MOME University
My role
Solo · research, service & UX/UI design
Timeline
One semester

Problem: Help exists in Budapest, but it's hard to find

In my first semester at MOME University Interaction design we got the brief: “how might we make vulnerable groups in cities safer and more supported during crisis”. The first thing that popped into my mind was period poverty, because I just attended an event by A hang, an activist group in Hungary, and I was shocked by the things I heard there. I knew that period poverty is a thing and it is present in Hungary, but I would have never thought how shocking the numbers and statistics are. So I started doing my first semester work on this topic.

What started as a broad social problem narrowed, through research, into a much sharper one: support already exists across Budapest, but it's scattered, quiet, and nearly impossible to find in the moment it's needed.

Locus is the product of this research. A citywide network of support points, education, and community, built around one habit: turning occasional generosity into a consistent, findable system of care.

Research

I wanted to understand the whole picture, so I started my research first worldwide, then Europe, and in the end I narrowed it down to Hungary. The bigger perspective helped me a lot to understand what the good practices are in other countries (like Scotland) and what Hungary is currently lacking to solve this problem.

Roughly 210,000 to 315,000 women and adolescents

Affected by period poverty. That is 10-15% of Hungary’s menstruators

Landscape

Hungary isn't starting from zero.

Before designing anything, I mapped what already exists: annual awareness campaigns like #nemluxustáska and the MENSZ campaign, institutional programs such as the Red Cross's "Lányból Nővé" (Girl to Woman), and local district actions like Józsefváros's municipal initiatives. The support is real, the question was why so few people seemed to know it existed.

After the desktop research I decided to conduct some interviews so I can map out this problem and get a first-hand understanding of it. I defined 5 stakeholder groups, and the plan was to talk with at least one person from each stakeholder group.

I made 11 interviews across lived-experience participants, professionals, policymakers, researchers, and grassroots activists.

After completing and analyzing the interviews I came to the conclusion:

Help exists in Hungary but it’s invisible.

Meaning, people in need often don’t know where to seek help, or that there is help somewhere for them at all. Also, current support is often seasonal (e.g., #nemluxustáska campaigns around holidays) or limited to specific schools (Red Cross), leaving women without coverage during other months.

Now that I knew what the actual root problem was, I started asking questions:

  • HMW turn scattered period poverty support into a coordinated citywide network?
  • HMW make menstrual help easily accessible and findable in Budapest?
  • HMW turn occasional donations into a consistent monthly habit of care?

I also stated my goal: Turn scattered period poverty support into a coordinated citywide network.

Solution

That’s how I decided to make:

A community-driven platform designed to ease menstrual poverty in Budapest while empowering women to become more conscious, informed, and connected.

Because I wanted to design a permanent solution, I started doing research on how to make a living community.

  • Foundation: Trust and Safety
  • Shared Purpose: Education and Empowerment
  • Activation: Participation and Visibility

Based on my findings I started planning the features to enable this community to work well.

  • Find
  • Learn
  • Community
  • Contribute

Made the information architecture and activity diagrams and started doing the wireframes.

The final product

Onboarding

Find your safe space

At its core, the app bridges the gap between those in need and a network of certified Support Points, where people in need can access the necessary products and help.

We are building more than a digital tool; we are constructing a permanent infrastructure of care.

Learn about yourself

A lot of menstruators still don’t know a lot of things about their cycle. In the Learn Hub, they can access basic information and articles from healthcare professionals.

Connect with others

There is no better way to keep an initiative alive than forming a strong community around it. Here people can see events related to cycle health, discuss topics, and ask for recommendations or help.

Contribute to the community

People will be the most engaged if they feel that they could actively help and contribute, so there is an option to support the community with donations and volunteering.

Impact

Locus is still a concept. It was a one-semester university project and hasn't launched, so I can't show usage numbers. What I can show is how the project changed my thinking.

The biggest shift came from the interviews. I started out wanting to design more help, but the people I talked to showed me that the help already exists. What's missing is the link between that help and the people who need it. That turned Locus from a donation app into a network that makes existing support visible and easy to find.

Talking to 11 people from 5 different groups taught me more than any desk research. Designing for a sensitive topic also taught me to think about trust and privacy before features.

If I continue the project, my next steps are to test the prototype with people with lived experience, ask a few organisations whether they would join as Support Points, and work out how the network could keep running long term.
